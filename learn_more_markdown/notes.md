# The Fall of Rome

In order for knowledge of any subject matter to grow, it is necessary for a strong societal foundation to be laid, otherwise members of the general public that would otherwise be capable of making groundbreaking discoveries would not have the foundations to do so. For instance, if someone doesn't have access to learning algebra, it would be far more difficult to make discoveries in calculus. This example illustrates how in particular, discoveries in the world of math are built based on the prior discoveries, and so any individual who has access to the prior discoveries has an inherent academic advantage. When the Roman Empire fell in 476 AD, along with it went institutional structure, including tax-funded academic institutions. This led to a dark age in European history characterized by slow academic progress and mathematical advancements.

# Texas Instruments

Texas Instruments (TI) is a major American technology company known for its long-standing role in the development of electronics, semiconductors, and educational calculators. Founded in 1930 and based in Texas, the company originally worked in geophysical exploration before shifting its focus toward electronics and integrated circuits during the mid-20th century, when semiconductor technology began transforming computing and communications. One of Texas Instruments’ most influential contributions to education and engineering has been its line of scientific and graphing calculators, such as the TI-30 and TI-84 series. These devices became widely adopted in schools and universities, standardizing how students learn algebra, calculus, statistics, and other mathematical fields. By making advanced computation accessible and portable, TI calculators helped shift education away from manual methods like slide rules and toward digital computation tools.


# Gentrification & Redlining

In the 1930s, the Home Owners' Loan Corporation created residential security maps that graded neighborhoods based on perceived financial risk. Areas with large Black or immigrant populations were frequently marked in red, a designation that signaled “hazardous” investment conditions. This practice, later known as redlining, led banks and insurers to deny mortgages and financial services to entire communities, resulting in decades of economic disinvestment. Because public schools in the United States are largely funded through local property taxes, these patterns of disinvestment had direct consequences for education. Redlined neighborhoods, with lower property values and reduced tax bases, often had underfunded schools, fewer resources, larger class sizes, and limited access to advanced coursework. In contrast, higher-rated neighborhoods accumulated wealth and were able to support better-funded school systems, reinforcing disparities in educational opportunity. Following the passage of the Fair Housing Act, explicit housing discrimination was outlawed, but the structural effects of redlining remained. In the late 20th and early 21st centuries, many of these same neighborhoods became targets for redevelopment. Rising property values brought new investment and, in some cases, improvements to local schools. However, these changes often came alongside increased housing costs that displaced long-term residents. As a result, families who had endured decades of under-resourced schools were frequently unable to benefit from the improvements that followed. Gentrification, in this context, not only reshapes neighborhoods but also redistributes access to quality education—continuing a cycle in which where a student lives remains one of the strongest predictors of the education they receive.


# COVID-19 Pandemic

From 2020 to 2022, most schools in the developed world closed in-person operations due to the global spread of COVID-19. In an effort to provide consistent access to education, schools shifted to online learning using video conferencing apps, namely Zoom. Despite their best efforts, this largely had a disastrous impact on K–12 education and led to significant learning loss, particularly in mathematics, where students rely heavily on guided practice and immediate feedback. Gaps widened between students with access to stable technology and support systems and those without, further exacerbating existing educational inequalities. Post-secondary education also suffered immensely; many students reported decreased engagement, limited access to campus resources, and a decline in the overall quality of instruction, particularly in courses requiring collaboration, laboratory work, or hands-on problem-solving. Graduation timelines were disrupted, and retention rates declined at many institutions. Teachers in the years that followed the pandemic reported significant challenges in the learning environment, such as reduced student attention spans, lower baseline skill levels, increased academic dishonesty facilitated by online tools, and greater difficulty maintaining classroom engagement and discipline. Analysts such as Thomas Kane claim that these issues were rising in the years leading up to 2020 and that the pandemic did not create them, but rather accelerated and exposed existing weaknesses within educational systems.
