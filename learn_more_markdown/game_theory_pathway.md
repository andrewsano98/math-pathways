


# Advanced Game Theory

## Nash Equilibrium in Strategic Systems

A Nash equilibrium is a state where no player can improve their outcome by changing strategy unilaterally. It represents a stable configuration of strategies in multi-agent systems.

--- PAGE ---

## Mixed Strategies and Probabilistic Decision-Making

Instead of choosing a single action, players can randomize over strategies. Mixed strategies use probability distributions to create equilibrium in games with no pure-strategy solution.

--- PAGE ---

## Bayesian Games and Incomplete Information

In many real systems, players do not know all relevant information. Bayesian games model this uncertainty using beliefs, priors, and conditional probabilities over hidden states.

--- PAGE ---

## Sequential Games and Dynamic Strategy Trees

In sequential games, players move in order rather than simultaneously. These systems are analyzed using game trees and backward induction to determine optimal strategies.

--- PAGE ---

## Subgame Perfect Equilibrium

A refinement of Nash equilibrium, subgame perfect equilibrium requires optimal strategies at every stage of a sequential game, ensuring consistency across all possible subgames.

--- PAGE ---

## Repeated Games and Strategy Evolution

When games are played multiple times, strategies evolve based on history. Cooperation, punishment, and reputation emerge through repeated interaction dynamics.

--- PAGE ---

## Evolutionary Game Theory and Fitness Dynamics

Strategies can spread through populations based on relative success rather than conscious choice. This connects game theory to biological and evolutionary systems.

--- PAGE ---

## Mechanism Design and Incentive Engineering

Mechanism design works backward from desired outcomes to construct rules that incentivize agents to behave in specific ways. This is “reverse game theory.”

--- PAGE ---

## Auctions and Optimal Bidding Strategies

Auction theory studies how rational agents bid under different formats (English, Dutch, sealed-bid). Optimal bidding depends on valuation, competition, and information structure.

--- PAGE ---

## Zero-Sum and Non-Zero-Sum Structures

In zero-sum games, one player’s gain is another’s loss. Non-zero-sum games allow mutual benefit or mutual loss, producing richer equilibrium structures.

--- PAGE ---

## Pareto Efficiency and Social Optimality

A state is Pareto efficient if no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off. This is a constraint-based optimality condition in multi-agent systems.

--- PAGE ---

## Correlated Equilibria and External Signals

Players can coordinate strategies using shared signals or correlations, leading to outcomes that outperform independent mixed strategies.

--- PAGE ---

## Stackelberg Competition and Leader–Follower Models

In hierarchical games, one player commits to a strategy first and others respond. This introduces asymmetry and anticipatory optimization.

--- PAGE ---

## Prisoner’s Dilemma and Cooperation Paradoxes

The Prisoner’s Dilemma shows how individually rational choices can lead to collectively suboptimal outcomes, highlighting tension between local and global optimization.

--- PAGE ---

## Evolution of Cooperation and Stable Strategies

Certain strategies persist over time due to stability against invasion by alternatives. This links game theory to dynamical systems and evolutionary stability concepts.

--- PAGE ---

## Information Asymmetry and Strategic Advantage

When players have unequal information, strategic advantage emerges. This leads to signaling, screening, and deception models in equilibrium analysis.
