# Introduction

Before mathematics became a formal discipline, it existed as a necessity. Long before symbols,
equations, or written language, humans were already engaging with the most fundamental 
mathematical idea: distinguishing "one" from "many,” and recording that distinction in a
stable form.

Counting represents the earliest stage of mathematical cognition in which the world is no
longer perceived solely in qualitative terms, but is instead organized through discrete
quantity. This transition marks a foundational shift in human reasoning: reality becomes
something that can be measured, stored, and compared.

The earliest known evidence of counting predates written language by tens of thousands
of years. Archaeological artifacts such as notched bones and tally marks suggest that
early humans developed systems for recording quantity as a form of external memory.
These systems were not abstract in origin, but practical—likely used for tracking time,
resources, or social obligations.

As societies became more complex, counting evolved from simple notational marks into structured
numerical systems embedded within early civilizations. Mesopotamian and Egyptian cultures
developed formalized arithmetic practices tied to administration, agriculture, and construction.
These systems represent a shift from personal record-keeping to institutional mathematics.

Across multiple independent civilizations, including those in Mesoamerica, China, and the
Andes, distinct counting systems emerged. Despite differences in representation—ranging from
positional notation to knotted cords—the underlying function remained consistent: to encode
quantity in a reproducible and communicable form.

A crucial development in the history of counting was the introduction of positional number
systems and the later formalization of zero. These innovations transformed counting from a
static recording method into a dynamic symbolic system capable of expressing arbitrarily
large quantities and enabling the development of higher mathematics.

In modern terms, counting is not merely an elementary skill but the foundational structure
upon which all quantitative disciplines depend. Fields such as economics, engineering,
data science, and computer science all trace their conceptual lineage back to the act of
enumeration.

Across cultures over history, the simplest method of addition and multiplication is by taking a number, and moving a component of a device a certain distance away from the starting number. This is similar to using your fingers to add - if you start with three fingers up, and put up four more, you can see seven fingers, hence 3 + 4 = 7.

# Tallies & Tally Sticks

Tally marks are one of the earliest known methods humans used to keep track of numbers, functioning as a simple system where each mark represents a single unit. Over time, people developed the habit of grouping these marks to make counting easier, most commonly in sets of five. This led to the familiar pattern of four vertical strokes with a fifth diagonal slash crossing them, creating an easily readable bundle that could be quickly summed. This system can be understood as a form of unary counting, since each mark corresponds directly to one item. Although later number systems such as binary and decimal became far more efficient for computation and abstraction, tally marks remain useful even today for keeping small running totals due to their simplicity and immediacy. Different cultures independently developed similar grouping conventions. In Western Europe, groups of five were often marked with a diagonal slash across four vertical lines. In China, a comparable system used the character 正 to represent five counts, while other traditions used boxed or crossed patterns for clarity. Early precursors to tally-based counting can be seen in artifacts such as the Ishango Bone, the Wolf bone, and the Lebombo bone, which contain sequences of notches that suggest early numerical recording practices long before formal writing systems emerged.

# Ishango Bone

The Ishango Bone, widely believed to be the oldest known mathematical artifact in existence, is the fibula of a baboon discovered in what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo. It was unearthed in 1950 by Jean de Heinzelin de Braucourt and is now housed in the Royal Belgian Institute of Natural Sciences. What makes this object remarkable is the series of notches carved into its surface, arranged into three distinct columns that appear to follow deliberate numerical patterns. One column is often interpreted as displaying prime numbers between 10 and 20, another reflects a duplication or doubling pattern, and a third suggests a structure reminiscent of a base ten system, including the numbers 9, 11, 19, and 21. Some researchers have proposed that the bone may have functioned as a primitive lunar calendar used to track menstrual cycles, and if this interpretation is correct, it raises the compelling possibility that some of the earliest mathematical thinkers were women.

# Abacus

The abacus is a manual calculating tool that uses beads arranged on rods to perform arithmetic on a large scale. By moving beads along these rods, users can quickly add, subtract, and even perform more advanced calculations depending on the number of columns and the system in use. It functions as a physical representation of place value, allowing numbers to be manipulated efficiently by hand. It was so effective that its use lasted from ancient times, strong into the 1980s, only being largely displaced by digital calulcators. Even with the invention of other computation tools, it still functioned immaculately as a tool for advanced counting, addition and subtraction. There are many variations such as the  Chinese Suanpan (2/5 beads), Japanese Soroban (1/4 beads), Russian Schoty (10 beads/row). Among these, the Soroban is one of the most widely recognized forms, where one upper bead represents the value five and the four lower beads each represent one unit, reflecting a clear structure for base-10 computation.


# Zu Chongzhi

Zu Chongzhi was a Chinese mathematician and astronomer of the 5th century, living during the Liu Song and Southern Qi dynasties. He is best known for producing an exceptionally accurate approximation of pi, calculating it to between 3.1415926 and 3.1415927, a level of precision that would not be surpassed for nearly a thousand years. This value was obtained through careful geometric methods involving inscribed and circumscribed polygons, extending earlier techniques used in classical Greek mathematics. In addition to his work on pi, Zu Chongzhi also contributed to calendar reform, improving the accuracy of astronomical timekeeping systems used to predict celestial cycles and seasonal changes. Zu Chongzhi’s legacy is often highlighted as an example of early high-precision computation and practical use, demonstrating that sophisticated numerical analysis existed well before the development of modern calculus or symbolic mathematics. Chinese math, alongside exchanges with India, enriched the western world's understanding of mathematics and built a strong foundations that laid the foundations for discoveries in the Enlightenment era.


# Quipu

The Inca used cotton strings tied with knots as a counting device in the Andes mountains in what is now modern Peru, Ecuador, Chile, Argentina and Colombia. These strings were called quipu, and . The quipu are very well preserved due to the arid climate of South America's west coast. These show the existence of a developed culture that kept data despite having no access to paper-like materials that Eurasia and Africa had access to, like papyrus. This system was a unique and brilliant adaptation to developing a number system, given the limitations the Inca's environment gave them that Eurasia and Africa did not face, such as living in a region of higher altitude and having never invented the wheel. After the Spanish Conquistors invaded, most of the civilization were wiped out by disease, and an impending civil war following the death of their leader. For hundreds of years, their system of numberskeeping was lost until it was rediscovered in 1912 by Leslie Leland Locke.

A first string was tied in a loop with many strings tied to it. A row of knots separated by gaps were used to represent numbers, typically three digit numbers. One final string would be tied to the first and last branching strings of focus, with the sum of the previous knots tied to that string, demonstrating a unique system of addition. This system was likely used for census information, accounting and measurements. This system resembled the decimal system that was invented in the Islamic world several hundred years earlier.


# Napier's Bones

Despite its name, and the fact that we have seen multiple instances of actual bones used for counting, Napier's bones are not actual bones, but metal rods with engravings of numbers with indexes. The rods are placed in a wooden tray and are used as a multiplication and division table for numbers beginning in 2 through 9. While simple calculations can be done mentally, Napier’s bones become especially useful when working with larger numbers, particularly those with four or more digits. The user aligns rods corresponding to each digit of a number, and the product is determined by reading across rows and summing values along diagonals, which indicate place value. This method reduces complex multiplication into a series of simpler additions. Invented by the mathematician John Napier in 1617, the device represents an important step in the development of computational tools. Like many such instruments, its practical use declined with the advent of mechanical and later digital calculators, but it remains a significant milestone in the history of computation


# Pascaline

Similar in concept to the abacus, the pascaline was designed as a computation device for the addition and subtraction of large numbers. Blaise Pascal, the machine's inventor, designed the pascaline for his father, who was a tax collector. The abacus and pascaline are similar in structure in that each column represents a numeric digit, and that addition is performed by moving a gear or bead in correspondence to the numbers the user is adding. That being said, the pascaline can be imagined as a more complex, gear-operated variant of the abacus. Both devices are essentially considered the first calculators, depending on the standard the user is willing to apply to each definition.


# Binary Number System

George Boole was a 19th-century mathematician who fundamentally changed the way logic could be expressed mathematically. He developed what is now called Boolean algebra, a system in which logical statements are reduced to binary values—true or false, represented today as 1 and 0. This work became one of the intellectual foundations of modern computing, because it showed that logical reasoning itself could be encoded into mathematical rules. Every digital circuit, from simple switches to complex processors, ultimately relies on principles derived from Boole’s system.

Digital computers process electronic signals in a series of "on" and "off," or essentially "electrical signal detected" or "no signal detected." This inherent nature means that computers process large information as a series of signals, known as bits. One bit alone may have two potential meanings, but two bits can have four meanings (00, 01, 10, and 11). Three signals can have 8 possible combinations (000, 001, 010, ... 111). Therefore, as the number of signals increases, we double the possible combinations associated with the last number of signals.

This exponential structure allows computers to represent and process all forms of digital information using only two states, forming the true foundation of modern computing systems.


# Hexadecimal Number System

In order to represent shades of color on a computer, we need a system where we can easily distinguish small numbers associated with red, green, and blue that the computer can convert on the backend into bits. Hexadecimal numbers do this quite well, since this numbering system has a base of 16. The first ten symbols are represented with numbers 0–9, and the following six are represented with letters A–F, corresponding to values 10–15.

Each hexadecimal digit represents exactly four binary bits, meaning any two-digit hexadecimal value corresponds precisely to one byte of computer memory, or 8 bits. This alignment is not arbitrary—it reflects the underlying structure of digital systems, where information is stored and processed in binary form but must often be interpreted by humans in a more compact and readable way.

Though its most visible application is in computer graphics, its use extends beyond color systems. Hexadecimal is widely used in memory addressing, debugging, machine code representation, and low-level system design. Its importance lies in its ability to compress binary information into a format that remains structurally faithful to the computer’s internal logic while becoming significantly more usable for humans. In this way, hexadecimal functions as a bridge between human cognition and the binary foundation of modern computing.


# Jefferson Disk

In the late 18th century, the United States was in the midst of the Revolutionary War, and the Americans were trying to find a way to push the British out of power. The monarchy was stationed in many large cities, including New York City, Boston, Yorktown, and Philadelphia. This meant that the revolutionairies had to be careful when communicating correspondence. Mail carriers could easily read sensitive information sent to allies, or the enemy can intercept the messages through theft, intimidation, or spying. This led to founding father Thomas Jefferson inventing the Jefferson Disk, also known as the wheel cipher. The disk was a cylinder with letters etched on moving segments, and an encoded message would need to be spelled out on the wheel that only allies had. Once they assembled the encoded message, they would then need to look for the hidden message in the disk to decipher the code. This crude form of cryptography was forgotten in history until it was inadvertently reinvented by Etienne Baxeries which was adapted by the US Army in World War I and World War II.


# Slide Rule


Despite the clear value digital calculators hold, there are some clear advantages to using a slide rule. Firstly, it forces the user to estimate a value using appropriate significant figures, unlike a calculator which will give you a value to many figures that are of no meaningful value. It also makes the scale of values much clearer. Finally, it also prevents mechanical errors such as missed-button inputs. Error reduction is overall the clearest advantage of the slide rule; it has been useful for these devices to the point where they were the calculation devices used in the Apollo mission. Of the prior analog calculators, this is considered the most useful, though it is still considered less practical than digital calculators given how quickly and easily it has become not only for digital calculators to not only perform arithmetic quickly, but for scientists and technicians to verify their findings through structural checks and balances. However there are still advantages, particularly in the field of engineering where significant figure errors can have dire consequences.


# Stepped Reckoner

By the 1600s, one of the most widely used tools for speeding up multiplication and division was Napier’s bones. While useful, it was still fundamentally a manual system: users relied on prewritten multiplication tables and performed the final addition steps by hand. As a result, it improved efficiency but did not remove the need for human calculation. During the early Enlightenment period, mathematicians increasingly relied on faster and more systematic ways of handling large numerical computations, especially in astronomy, navigation, and emerging scientific fields. One of the earliest attempts to mechanize this process was the Stepped Reckoner, invented by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. Unlike Napier’s bones, which assisted human calculation, the Stepped Reckoner attempted to automate arithmetic itself. It used a stepped-drum gear system that allowed repeated mechanical addition and subtraction through a hand crank, enabling multiplication and division to be carried out as physical processes rather than mental ones. While not widely adopted in practice, it represented an important conceptual shift: computation could be delegated to a machine following fixed mechanical rules. In this way, it marked an early step toward the idea that complex arithmetic could be automated, a principle that would later become foundational in the development of mechanical calculators and modern computing systems. Although in practice, the device was rarely used as it was considered expensive and impractical to build.


# Analytical Engine

The Analytical Engine was a proposed mechanical computer designed in the 1830s that is often considered the first conceptual model of a general-purpose computer. Unlike earlier machines that performed only fixed arithmetic operations, it was designed to follow programmable instructions using punched cards, similar to those used in textile looms. It included components analogous to modern computers: a “mill” for processing (CPU), a “store” for memory, and a system for input and output. Although never fully built in its time, its design introduced the idea that machines could be programmable and not limited to a single task.


# Difference Engine

The Difference Engine was an earlier invention by Charles Babbage intended to automatically compute mathematical tables, particularly polynomial and logarithmic tables. It was designed to eliminate human error in repetitive calculations by using gears and mechanical steps to compute and print results automatically. While only partially built during Babbage’s lifetime, it demonstrated that complex mathematical tables could be generated by a machine rather than manual calculation, marking an important step toward automated computation.


# Decimal System

The decimal system, also known as the base-10 system, is the foundation of modern numerical representation and computation. It originated in ancient India, where mathematicians developed a place-value system using the digits 0–9. This knowledge was later preserved, expanded, and transmitted by scholars of the medieval Islamic world, particularly during the Islamic Golden Age. Figures such as Al-Khwarizmi played a key role in formalizing and disseminating these methods through influential texts on arithmetic. From there, the system spread into Europe, where it gradually replaced older numeral systems like Roman numerals. Its efficiency in calculation—especially when combined with the concept of zero—revolutionized mathematics, commerce, and science. Today, the decimal system underpins everything from everyday counting to advanced scientific and technological work.


# Roman Numerals

As western Europe grew a more advanced economy and political state, people needed a method of keeping track of numbers in a more advanced way than with a simple unary system of tallies. This led to the development of what is now a largely outdated system of counting, the Roman numeral system. This method was considered flawed in several ways: there was no symbol for 0, and there was no straightforward method to count beyond 3999. Roman numerals are a number system developed in ancient Rome that uses combinations of letters (I, V, X, L, C, D, M) to represent values. Instead of a place-value system like modern decimals, numbers are built through additive and subtractive patterns (for example, IV = 4, IX = 9). While widely used in trade, architecture, and recordkeeping, the system is not well-suited for complex arithmetic because it lacks a zero and place-value structure. As a result, calculations were often performed using external tools rather than directly with the numerals themselves.


# Chinese Counting Rods

Chinese counting rods were a physical numerical system used for calculation in ancient China. Small rods were arranged on a counting surface to represent digits in a place-value system, allowing addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division to be performed visually and systematically. Unlike written numeral systems, this method enabled direct manipulation of numbers in a structured grid format. It is considered one of the earliest fully developed positional calculation systems and an important precursor to later computational methods.


# Rhind Mathematical Papyrus

The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus is an ancient Egyptian document dating to around 1550 BCE that contains a collection of mathematical problems and solutions. It includes methods for arithmetic, fractions, geometry, and practical problem-solving related to land measurement and construction. Written by the scribe Ahmes, it demonstrates that ancient Egyptian mathematics was highly practical and systematic, focused on solving real-world administrative and engineering problems rather than abstract theory.


# Babylonian Clay Tablets

Babylonian clay tablets were used in ancient Mesopotamia to record numerical data using cuneiform writing. The Babylonian number system was base-60 (sexagesimal), allowing for highly structured calculations, particularly in astronomy and trade. These tablets contain some of the earliest known mathematical records, including multiplication tables and geometric calculations. They demonstrate that advanced numerical reasoning and systematic computation existed thousands of years before modern mathematics.


# The Introduction of 0

The introduction of zero as both a placeholder and a number in its own right was one of the most profound developments in the history of mathematics. While early placeholder concepts appeared in ancient civilizations such as Babylon, it was mathematicians in India who, by the 5th century, formalized zero as a number with defined arithmetic properties. This innovation allowed for more efficient calculation, enabled the development of algebra, and laid the foundation for modern number systems. Without zero, place value notation and much of higher mathematics would be impossible.


# Mayan Codices

The Mayan Codices are among the few surviving written records of the Maya civilization, offering valuable insight into their advanced mathematical and astronomical knowledge. Created between the 12th and 15th centuries, these bark-paper manuscripts reveal a sophisticated vigesimal (base-20) number system that included a symbol for zero—developed independently of other cultures. The codices contain detailed calendrical calculations, eclipse predictions, and astronomical tables, demonstrating a deep understanding of cyclical patterns and long-term timekeeping.


# Tally Stones

Stones were among the earliest tools used for counting and recordkeeping. By placing or grouping stones, early humans could track quantities such as livestock, food supplies, or time intervals. This system represents a form of external memory, where physical objects substitute for abstract numerical symbols. Although extremely simple, it reflects one of the earliest steps toward structured mathematical thinking: representing quantity outside the human mind.
