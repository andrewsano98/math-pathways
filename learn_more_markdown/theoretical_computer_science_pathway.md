


# Theoretical Computer Science

## Computability and Decidability

Theoretical computer science studies which problems can be solved by algorithms at all. Some problems are decidable (a solution exists), while others are undecidable, meaning no algorithm can solve them in all cases.

--- PAGE ---

## Turing Machines and Formal Computation Models

A Turing machine is an abstract model of computation that manipulates symbols on a tape using rules. It formalizes what it means for a function to be computable and underpins modern computation theory.

--- PAGE ---

## Church–Turing Thesis and Limits of Computation

The Church–Turing thesis states that anything computable by an algorithm can be computed by a Turing machine. It defines the boundary between computable and non-computable problems.

--- PAGE ---

## Complexity Classes and Computational Difficulty

Problems are grouped into classes like P, NP, and PSPACE based on how resource requirements scale. This categorizes not just solvability, but efficiency under constraints.

--- PAGE ---

## P vs NP Problem and Verification vs Search

The P vs NP question asks whether every problem whose solution can be verified quickly can also be solved quickly. This separates “easy to check” from “easy to find.”

--- PAGE ---

## Reductions and Problem Transformations

Reductions transform one problem into another to compare difficulty. If a hard problem reduces to another, that problem inherits the same computational hardness.

--- PAGE ---

## Automata Theory and Formal Languages

Automata (finite machines, pushdown automata, etc.) recognize patterns in strings. Formal languages define structured sets of valid inputs and their grammatical rules.

--- PAGE ---

## Regular Expressions and Language Hierarchies

Languages are classified by complexity into hierarchies (regular, context-free, context-sensitive). Each level corresponds to different computational power.

--- PAGE ---

## NP-Completeness and Universal Hardness

NP-complete problems are those that are both in NP and as hard as any problem in NP. Solving one efficiently would solve all NP problems efficiently.

--- PAGE ---

## Proof Theory and Formal Verification

Proof systems encode mathematical reasoning into formal symbolic steps. This allows verification of correctness in algorithms, software, and logical systems.

--- PAGE ---

## Gödel Incompleteness and System Limitations

Gödel’s theorems show that in any sufficiently powerful logical system, there are true statements that cannot be proven within the system itself.

--- PAGE ---

## Randomness and Probabilistic Computation

Some algorithms use randomness to achieve efficiency or simplicity. Probabilistic computation studies what can be solved with high probability rather than certainty.

--- PAGE ---

## Quantum Computation and Extended Models

Quantum computing extends classical computation using quantum states, superposition, and interference. It changes complexity boundaries for certain classes of problems.

--- PAGE ---

## Algorithmic Information Theory and Kolmogorov Complexity

This field measures the complexity of objects based on the shortest program that can produce them. It connects computation with compression and randomness.

--- PAGE ---

## Interactive Proof Systems and Verification Protocols

Some proofs can be verified through interaction rather than full derivation. This leads to classes like IP and probabilistically checkable proofs (PCPs).
